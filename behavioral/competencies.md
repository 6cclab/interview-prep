# Behavioral Competencies

A map of what behavioral interviewers are actually probing, pitched at staff
scope. The bar at this level is not "worked well on a team" — it's influence
without authority, setting technical direction, escalating a pattern instead
of patching an instance, and owning outcomes that cross team boundaries.

Each competency below covers: what's actually being tested, what a strong
answer contains, the common way candidates blow it, and what separates a
staff-level answer from a senior one. This is a map, not a script — the
stories that fill it in live elsewhere.

---

## Conflict / disagreement with a peer

**What's being tested.** Whether you can hold a technical position under
social pressure without becoming either a pushover or a steamroller. The
interviewer wants to see how you handle disagreement when there's no
hierarchy to resolve it — just two people who think the other is wrong.

**What a strong answer contains.** A real disagreement where the other
person's position was defensible, not a straw man. A clear account of how you
tried to understand their reasoning before pushing back. A resolution
mechanism that didn't depend on you outlasting or outranking them — data,
a prototype, a narrowed scope, a third party, or a genuine change of mind on
either side. What the working relationship looked like afterward.

**The common failure mode.** Picking a conflict where the candidate was
obviously right and the other person was obviously unreasonable. This tests
nothing — it's not a conflict, it's a story about someone else being wrong.
The tell is that the "resolution" is just the candidate winning without
having to update their own view at all.

**What makes it staff-level.** The disagreement is usually about direction or
tradeoffs, not implementation detail — architecture, ownership boundaries,
what to build at all. A staff answer shows the candidate treating the
relationship and the org's ability to disagree well as part of the outcome,
not just the technical resolution. Often the candidate changed their mind on
part of it, or the resolution left both people better calibrated for next
time.

## Disagreeing with a decision from above, and what you did after the decision stood

**What's being tested.** Whether you can push back on someone with more
authority than you, lose the argument, and then execute in good faith rather
than sandbag, litigate in private, or quietly do it your way anyway. This is
as much about the "after" as the disagreement itself.

**What a strong answer contains.** A decision you genuinely thought was
wrong, made by someone with the authority to make it. How you raised the
disagreement — once, clearly, with your reasoning, at the right time, not as
a running complaint. What information or perspective they had that you
didn't, if any. And critically: what you did once the decision stood —
executed it as if it were your own idea, flagged risks along the way without
relitigating, and what happened as a result, including if you turned out to
be right.

**The common failure mode.** Answers that stop at "I raised my concerns" and
skip the part where the decision didn't go their way. Or answers where the
candidate technically complied but the story makes clear they undermined it,
said "I told you so" when it went wrong, or never really let go of being
right. Also common: a "disagreement" that was actually resolved in the
candidate's favor, which answers a different question.

**What makes it staff-level.** Staff engineers get overruled by people with
less technical context than them fairly often — that's what it means to not
have unilateral authority over direction. The staff-level move is separating
"I was right" from "I executed well," and being able to point to a case where
those diverged. Bonus signal: whether the candidate built the disagreement
into something durable afterward — a documented tradeoff, a revisit
trigger, a retro — rather than just letting it go.

## Failure

**What's being tested.** Self-awareness, ownership, and whether you actually
learned something durable — versus performing humility with a failure that
cost nothing and taught nothing.

**What a strong answer contains.** A real failure with a real cost: broken
trust, lost time, a wrong technical bet, damage that took effort to repair.
A clear, undiluted account of the candidate's own role in it — not "we
underestimated" but "I decided X and didn't check Y." What the cost actually
was, concretely. What changed afterward — in the candidate's judgment, in a
process, in how they check their own decisions now — that would plausibly
prevent a repeat.

**The common failure mode.** The "humble brag" failure — working too hard,
caring too much, a failure that's secretly a strength. Also common: diffusing
ownership across the team so the candidate's specific contribution to the
failure is never named. Or a failure with no real cost, which reads as
picking the safest possible story rather than an honest one.

**What makes it staff-level.** The failure is usually a judgment failure, not
an execution failure — a wrong call about scope, risk, architecture, or
priority, made with real authority to make that call. A staff answer
distinguishes between "I made a mistake" and "I had a flawed model of the
world, and here's specifically what was wrong with it and how I checked
myself against it since." The learning generalizes past the one incident.

## Influence without authority

**What's being tested.** Whether you can get an org to move in a direction
without a reporting line to force it — the core staff skill. This is
sometimes tested directly, sometimes folded into other questions.

**What a strong answer contains.** A goal that required people outside the
candidate's team or reporting chain to change what they were doing. How the
candidate built the case — technical merit alone rarely moves people, so look
for evidence of also addressing what other stakeholders cared about, timing,
sequencing, or trust built in advance. Concrete tactics: who they talked to
first, what artifact or demo made it real, how they handled the people who
weren't convinced. The actual outcome and what stuck versus what reverted
once the candidate stopped pushing.

**The common failure mode.** A story that's actually about authority the
candidate did have — convincing their own reports, or a decision that was
theirs to make anyway. Or an answer that's all technical merit with no
account of people: "the design was obviously better so people adopted it,"
which skips the actual skill being tested.

**What makes it staff-level.** Staff-level influence usually spans multiple
teams or an org boundary, and the mechanism is durable — a standard, a
default, a piece of shared infrastructure, a changed process — not a
one-time favor called in. The candidate can name specifically who was
skeptical and what changed their mind, not just "eventually people came
around."

## Operating under ambiguity

**What's being tested.** Whether you can make forward progress and produce a
useful outcome when the problem, the requirements, or the right approach
aren't defined yet — without either freezing or barreling ahead on a guess.

**What a strong answer contains.** A situation with a genuinely underspecified
starting point — not just "the ticket was vague" but something closer to a
real open question the org had. How the candidate scoped the ambiguity down:
what they decided to find out first, what assumptions they made explicit and
tested, how they avoided both paralysis and wasted motion on a wrong
direction. How they kept stakeholders oriented while the shape of the
problem was still moving. What they'd have done differently in hindsight.

**The common failure mode.** A story where the ambiguity was resolved by
someone else — the candidate waited for clarity rather than helping create
it. Or the opposite: charged ahead on a guess that turned out wrong, framed
as decisiveness, with no account of how they checked themselves along the
way.

**What makes it staff-level.** Staff engineers are often the ones the org
depends on to define the problem, not just solve a defined one. A staff
answer shows the candidate actively shaping the ambiguity — proposing a
frame, running a small experiment, writing the doc that becomes the shared
understanding — rather than navigating ambiguity that someone else created
and will eventually resolve.

## Mentoring and growing other engineers

**What's being tested.** Whether you multiply the org's output through other
people, and whether you can tell the difference between mentoring and doing
the work for someone.

**What a strong answer contains.** A specific person (described generically,
no names needed) and a specific gap — technical, or something more like
judgment, communication, or confidence. What the candidate actually did:
how they diagnosed the gap, what they did differently from just answering
questions or fixing things quietly, how they created space for the person to
struggle productively rather than rescuing them too fast. A concrete change
in what that person could do afterward, and how the candidate knows it
stuck rather than assuming it did.

**The common failure mode.** A story that's really about the candidate being
technically impressive in front of a junior engineer, with the junior
engineer as a prop. Or "mentoring" that was actually just doing the
person's work for them under a different name, which the candidate frames
as generosity.

**What makes it staff-level.** At staff level this usually isn't 1:1
mentoring inside a team — it's raising the level of an org: a review
practice, a template, a pattern taught once and then adopted broadly, or
identifying who has the potential to do something they don't yet believe
they can, and engineering the opportunity for them to find out. The
candidate can point to something that kept happening after they stopped
being directly involved.

## Setting technical direction

**What's being tested.** Whether you can define where a system or a
technical area should go, get buy-in for it, and see it through — as
distinct from executing well against direction someone else set.

**What a strong answer contains.** A real fork in the road — a system that
could plausibly evolve multiple ways, not a decision with one obvious
answer. How the candidate diagnosed that a decision needed to be made, who
they pulled in, what evidence or prototypes shaped the direction, and how
they got a group of people who didn't have to be persuaded to actually be
persuaded. What the direction cost in the short term and what it bought
later. Places where the direction had to be adjusted, and whether the
candidate noticed that themselves.

**The common failure mode.** A story that's actually about implementation
skill — building something well against a direction that was already
decided — dressed up as direction-setting. Or a direction that was
imposed rather than built consensus around, which may show authority but
doesn't show the influence and judgment the question is testing.

**What makes it staff-level.** The direction affects more than the
candidate's own team, and outlives the project that prompted it — it
becomes the default other people build against. A staff answer can name the
alternative seriously considered and why it lost, not just why the chosen
direction was right.

## Escalating a systemic problem rather than patching the local instance

**What's being tested.** Whether you notice when a bug, an incident, or a
complaint is actually a symptom of something structural, and whether you do
the harder, less immediately rewarding work of fixing the pattern instead of
closing the ticket.

**What a strong answer contains.** A specific local problem — an incident, a
recurring bug class, a repeated support burden — that the candidate
recognized as an instance of something bigger: a missing abstraction, an
ownership gap, an incentive that pushed people toward the wrong behavior
repeatedly. How they made the case that the systemic fix was worth the cost
over just patching the instance, especially when patching would have been
faster and easier. Who had to agree, what the systemic fix actually was, and
evidence that the pattern stopped recurring afterward — not just that this
one instance was resolved.

**The common failure mode.** A story that's actually just a good bug fix,
relabeled as "systemic" without evidence the same class of problem was
recurring, or without a fix that addressed the cause rather than the latest
symptom. The giveaway is that the "systemic fix" is the same kind of change
as the patch would have been, just applied once more broadly.

**What makes it staff-level.** This is one of the more distinctly staff
competencies — recognizing patterns across incidents or teams is exactly the
kind of horizontal visibility a staff engineer has that an individual
contributor on one team doesn't. A strong answer shows the candidate
connecting dots that weren't obviously connected, and choosing the slower,
structural fix on purpose, with a clear account of the tradeoff against
just patching it.

## Prioritisation and saying no

**What's being tested.** Whether you can protect scope, time, or quality
against pressure — from your own enthusiasm as much as from other people —
and do it in a way that doesn't just alienate whoever wanted the thing.

**What a strong answer contains.** A real ask that had a legitimate
constituency behind it — not a frivolous request that was easy to refuse.
How the candidate evaluated it against everything else in flight, what
tradeoff they made explicit, and how they communicated the no (or the
"not now") in a way that kept the relationship and the requester's trust
intact. What the requester actually got instead, if anything — a
smaller version, a later date, a different owner.

**The common failure mode.** A story where the ask being declined was
obviously unreasonable, so the "no" cost nothing and convinced no one who
mattered. Or a no delivered unilaterally with no account of how the other
side reacted or what they needed instead — which reads as protecting the
candidate's own time rather than making a real prioritization call.

**What makes it staff-level.** At staff level the no usually has a cost
attached that the candidate can state plainly — a stakeholder relationship
strained, a roadmap commitment reshuffled, a team's plan changed — and the
candidate owns having made that call rather than hiding behind "the team
decided." The strong version also shows the candidate revisiting the no
later rather than treating it as settled forever.

## Handling a production incident

**What's being tested.** Composure and judgment under real time pressure,
and whether the candidate treats an incident as a moment to also make the
system better afterward, not just get the lights back on.

**What a strong answer contains.** What actually broke and how the
candidate found out. The diagnosis process — what they checked first and
why, what red herrings they ruled out, how they decided between a fast
mitigation and a proper fix under pressure. How they communicated status
while the incident was live, especially if other people were blocked or
customers were affected. What the resolution was, how long it took, and
what changed afterward — monitoring, a runbook, an architectural fix — so
the same failure mode doesn't recur.

**The common failure mode.** A blow-by-blow technical narrative with no
account of communication, decision-making under uncertainty, or what
changed afterward — it reads as a debugging story, not an incident story.
Also common: no real stakes, so there's nothing to show composure against.

**What makes it staff-level.** Staff engineers are often the ones other
people look to during an incident to decide whether to escalate, who to
pull in, and when to stop firefighting and communicate instead of dig
further. A staff answer shows the candidate making those meta-decisions,
not just finding the bug fastest — and shows follow-through into a
structural fix, not just a postmortem document that nobody acted on.

## Working with product and design

**What's being tested.** Whether you can operate as a peer to non-engineering
partners — pushing back on infeasible asks, surfacing tradeoffs early, and
shaping what gets built rather than just building whatever spec arrives.

**What a strong answer contains.** A case where the initial ask from product
or design had a real technical or user cost the candidate could see and the
partner initially couldn't. How the candidate made that visible without
being obstructive — bringing options rather than just objections, engaging
with what the partner was actually trying to achieve rather than just the
literal spec. What the outcome looked like: a changed scope, a phased
approach, a different tradeoff everyone could live with. Evidence the
relationship stayed collaborative afterward.

**The common failure mode.** A story where engineering is right and
product/design is wrong, framed as a battle won — which reads as an
engineer who doesn't respect the other discipline's expertise, not as
collaboration. Also common: pure execution stories with no evidence the
candidate shaped anything upstream of "here's what was speced."

**What makes it staff-level.** Staff engineers are often in the room early
enough to shape the ask before it's a commitment, not just react to a
finished spec. A strong answer shows the candidate influencing product or
design thinking directly — reframing a problem, proposing an alternative
that better serves the underlying goal — and treating the partner's goals as
legitimate constraints to design around, not obstacles to route around.

## Why you're leaving / why this company

**What's being tested.** Whether your story is honest, self-aware, and
forward-looking — and whether it holds up under a follow-up question. This is
less about content and more about whether it's actually true and whether the
candidate can say it without either trashing the current employer or being
implausibly vague.

**What a strong answer contains.** A real, specific reason to move that's
about what the candidate wants next — scope, problem space, stage of
company, kind of work — rather than only what they're escaping. Enough
honesty about the current situation to be credible, without turning into
grievance. A reason for this company specifically that isn't generic
flattery — something concrete about the role, the problem, or the stage that
connects to what the candidate is looking for.

**The common failure mode.** Trashing the current employer, or implying it
by contrast — praising the target company in a way that only makes sense if
the current org is worse. Also common: an answer so diplomatic it says
nothing, which reads as evasive rather than professional. And a "why this
company" that's clearly copy-pasted enthusiasm unconnected to the specific
role.

**What makes it staff-level.** The reasoning is usually about scope and
leverage — the kind of problems available, the level of ownership on offer,
whether the org is at a stage where staff-level influence is actually needed
and possible — rather than a generic complaint about pace or politics. A
staff-level answer treats this as a real career decision with real tradeoffs
named, not a line to get past.
